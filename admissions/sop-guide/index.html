How to Write a Statement of Purpose (SOP) for Study Abroad

19 Nov 2026 · 7 min read · Last checked 19 Nov 2026

A statement of purpose (SOP) is the one document in your application where you speak for yourself. Transcripts show your grades; the SOP shows your thinking — why this subject, why this university, why now, and what you will do with the degree. Admissions officers read hundreds of them. Most are forgettable. This guide explains how to write one that is not.

Key facts

DetailWhat to know
LengthUsually 500–1,000 words (1–2 pages). Always check the university's limit — some ask for 500 words, others for 1,500, and UCAS personal statements are capped at 4,000 characters.
Who reads itAdmissions tutors and, for scholarships, selection panels — usually in a few minutes
What it must doConnect your past (study and work) to this specific programme and your future plans
What it is notNot a CV in sentences, not a life story, not a list of adjectives about yourself
OriginalityMust be your own writing. Universities check for plagiarism and AI-generated text, and copied SOPs are rejected

What admissions officers actually look for

An SOP answers four questions. If your draft does not answer all four, it is incomplete.

  1. Why this subject? What experience made you choose this field — a course, a project, a job, a problem you saw in Bangladesh? Be specific. "I have always been passionate about economics" tells the reader nothing. "Running a small survey of 40 shopkeepers in my district showed me how microcredit actually works" tells them something.
  2. Why this university and this programme? Name the programme, and one or two things about it — a module, a research group, a professor's work. This is the part that proves you did not send the same SOP to ten universities.
  3. Are you prepared? Show the academic background and skills that qualify you — the relevant courses, the project, the work experience — and be honest about gaps.
  4. What will you do with it? What is your plan after graduation? Admissions officers want students who know where they are going, especially for scholarships, where the funder wants to know what you will do for your home country.

The structure that works

A clear structure beats clever writing. Use this skeleton and fill it with your own specifics:

1. Opening (1 paragraph, 80–120 words)

Start with the moment or experience that led you to this field — not a quotation, not a dictionary definition, and not "Since I was a child…". One concrete scene or decision is enough. Example shape: "In my final year at Dhaka University, my dissertation on flood-resilient housing took me to three villages in Kurigram. The families I interviewed changed what I want to study."

2. Academic background (1–2 paragraphs)

What you studied, what you did well in, and what you learned — with specifics. Name the courses, the project, the grade if it helps. If your grades dipped in one semester, one honest sentence explaining why is better than silence. Do not repeat your CV line by line; pick the two or three academic experiences most relevant to this programme.

3. Experience and skills (1–2 paragraphs)

Work, internships, volunteering, research assistance — whatever connects to the programme. For each experience, say what you did and what it taught you, not just where you worked. "I interned at BRAC" is a line on a CV. "At BRAC I cleaned and analysed survey data from 2,000 households, and learned how field data differs from textbook data" is evidence.

4. Why this programme (1 paragraph)

This is the paragraph most applicants write worst, because they write it last and in general terms. Name the programme. Name a module, a research centre, or a professor whose work relates to yours. Explain what you will learn there that you cannot learn elsewhere. If you cannot name anything specific about the programme, you have not researched it enough.

5. Future plans (1 paragraph)

What you will do after the degree — short term (first job or role) and longer term. Be concrete: an organisation, a sector, a problem in Bangladesh you want to work on. For scholarship applications, this paragraph carries extra weight, because funders select people who will use the degree at home. Chevening, for example, selects future leaders on the strength of their career plans.

6. Closing (2–3 sentences)

One short paragraph that ties the story together and states clearly that you are ready for the programme. No new information, no grand claims.

Example outline (not a sample SOP)

The outline below shows the shape of a strong SOP. It is a structure to follow, not text to copy — an SOP written from a template is easy to recognise and easy to reject.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with a quotation or a childhood dream. "Since I was a child, I have dreamed of studying abroad" is the most common opening in the pile. It says nothing about you.
  • Listing adjectives instead of evidence. "I am hardworking, dedicated and passionate" is a claim. Evidence is a project you finished, a problem you solved, a result you produced.
  • Copying a sample SOP. Sample SOPs circulate widely, and admissions officers have seen them. Universities check for plagiarism, and a copied SOP is an automatic rejection — and can affect future applications.
  • One SOP for every university. The "why this programme" paragraph must be rewritten for each university. Sending the same SOP with the university name changed is obvious and insulting to the reader.
  • Too long, or too short. Ignoring the word limit is the easiest way to fail. If the limit is 500 words, write 500 words — not 900, not 200.
  • Bad English, unproofread. Spelling and grammar errors in an SOP undermine the whole application, especially when you are claiming strong English skills. Read it aloud, and ask someone else to read it too.
  • Blaming others for weak grades. If you explain a weak semester, do it in one honest sentence and move on. A paragraph of excuses makes it worse.
  • Writing what you think they want to hear. Admissions officers read thousands of SOPs. The ones that stand out are honest and specific, not the ones that sound most impressive.

Checklist before you submit

  • Does it answer all four questions: why this subject, why this university, am I prepared, what next?
  • Is it within the word or character limit?
  • Is the "why this programme" paragraph specific to this university?
  • Have you removed every adjective that is not backed by evidence?
  • Have you read it aloud, start to finish, to catch awkward sentences?
  • Has someone else read it — a teacher, a senior, a friend who will be honest?
  • Is every claim in it true and checkable?
  • Is it your own writing, in your own words?

A note on AI tools: using an AI tool to check your grammar is fine. Asking it to write your SOP is not — the result reads like every other AI-written SOP, and universities increasingly check for it. Your own voice, with your own specific experiences, is the one advantage no one else can copy.
