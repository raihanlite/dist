20 Common English Grammar Mistakes

August 21, 2026 by Study Koro · 9 min read

English grammar can be confusing, especially when two words or sentence structures look almost the same. Some mistakes are very common because people hear or use them in everyday conversation.

The good news is that most of these mistakes are easy to fix once you understand the rule.

Here are some common English grammar mistakes, with simple explanations and examples.

1. I Wish I Was vs. I Wish I Were

When you talk about an unreal or imaginary situation, use “were” in formal English.

Common: I wish I was a cat.

Better in formal English: I wish I were a cat.

Why? The sentence describes something that is not true. This is called the subjunctive mood.

More examples:

  • I wish I were taller.
  • I wish she were here.
  • If I were you, I would study harder.

In everyday conversation, “I wish I was” is also widely used, but “I wish I were” is the safer choice for formal writing and exams.

2. Giving an Exam vs. Taking an Exam

Students often say:

Incorrect in standard English: I gave an exam yesterday.

Correct: I took an exam yesterday.

A student takes or sits for an exam. A teacher gives, sets, or administers an exam.

For example:

  • I took my English exam yesterday.
  • The teacher gave us an English exam.
  • She is preparing to sit for her final exam.

3. Enter vs. Enter Into

The verb “enter” already means “go into,” so we normally do not use “to” after it when talking about entering a place.

Incorrect: We entered into the classroom.

Correct: We entered the classroom.

However, “enter into” is correct when it has a different meaning.

For example:

  • The two companies entered into an agreement.
  • They entered into a discussion about the problem.

So, remember:

Enter + place

Enter into + agreement, discussion, contract, etc.

4. Using “The” Before General Nouns and Languages

Do not use “the” before a material or substance when you are talking about it in general.

Incorrect: The gold is a precious metal.

Correct: Gold is a precious metal.

We also do not normally use “the” before the name of a language.

Incorrect: He speaks the English.

Correct: He speaks English.

More examples:

  • Water is important for life.
  • Silver is a useful metal.
  • She speaks Bengali and English.

There are exceptions. For example, “the English” can refer to English people as a group.

5. Returned vs. Returned Back

“Return” already means “go or come back,” so “back” is usually unnecessary.

Incorrect: She returned back to school.

Correct: She returned to school.

You can also say:

  • She went back to school.
  • She came back to school.

Do not use “returned back” when “returned” already expresses the meaning you need.

6. Between vs. Among

A common rule says to use “between” for two things and “among” for three or more. This is useful for beginners, but it is not the complete rule.

“Between” is generally used when we are talking about separate, clearly identified people or things. It can be used with more than two.

Examples:

  • The agreement was between Bangladesh, India, and Nepal.
  • The money was divided between the three brothers.

“Among” is usually used when people or things are considered as part of a group.

Examples:

  • She was sitting among her friends.
  • The teacher distributed the books among the students.

For Class 10 students, remember that “between” often focuses on individual relationships, while “among” focuses on a group.

7. Then vs. Than

“Than” is used when making a comparison.

Incorrect: I would rather have cake then ice cream.

Correct: I would rather have cake than ice cream.

“Then” is usually related to time or sequence.

Examples:

  • She is taller than me.
  • This book is better than that one.
  • We finished our homework and then watched television.

A simple trick:

Than = comparison

Then = time or next step

8. Fewer vs. Less

Use “fewer” with countable nouns.

Use “less” with uncountable nouns.

Incorrect: I have less books than you.

Correct: I have fewer books than you.

Examples:

  • There are fewer students in the class today.
  • I have fewer problems now.
  • I need less water.
  • We have less time.

You can count books, students, and apples. You cannot normally count water, time, or sugar as individual units.

So:

Fewer + countable nouns

Less + uncountable nouns

9. Its vs. It's

These two words have different meanings.

Its shows possession.

It's is a short form of “it is” or “it has.”

Incorrect: The cat licked it's paw.

Correct: The cat licked its paw.

Examples:

  • The dog hurt its leg.
  • The company changed its policy.
  • It's raining.
  • It's been a long day.

A useful test is to replace “it's” with “it is” or “it has.” If the sentence still makes sense, “it's” may be correct.

10. Avoid Double Negatives in Standard English

A double negative happens when two negative words are used in the same clause.

Non-standard: I don't need no help.

Standard: I don't need any help.

You can also say:

I need no help.

In some dialects and informal varieties of English, double negatives are used intentionally. However, they are generally avoided in formal English and academic writing.

11. Who vs. Whom

“Who” is normally used as the subject of a sentence.

“Whom” is used as the object.

Incorrect: Whom is calling?

Correct: Who is calling?

Here, “who” is the person doing the action of calling.

Compare:

  • Who called you?
  • Whom did you call?

A simple trick is to replace the word with “he” or “him.”

If “he” fits, use who.

If “him” fits, use whom.

In modern everyday English, “who” is often used instead of “whom,” especially in conversation. However, knowing the difference is useful for formal writing and exams.

12. Go Home vs. Go to Home

When “home” means your usual place of residence, we normally say “go home,” not “go to home.”

Incorrect: I want to go to home.

Correct: I want to go home.

Examples:

  • I went home after school.
  • She came home late.
  • They are going home now.

But we use “to” when “home” has a determiner or describes a specific building or place.

For example:

  • I went to my friend's home.
  • We went to their new home.

13. Center vs. Middle

“Center” and “middle” are sometimes interchangeable, but they are not always used in exactly the same way.

“Center” often refers to the central point or area of something.

Examples:

  • Draw a circle in the center of the page.
  • There is a table in the center of the room.

“Middle” is often used for a position between two sides or at an approximately central point.

Examples:

  • He was standing in the middle of the road.
  • She sat in the middle of the bus.
  • The school is in the middle of the village.

Do not treat “center” and “middle” as completely different words. Their meanings often overlap.

14. Customer vs. Client

“Customer” and “client” both refer to people who receive something from a business, but they are commonly used in different situations.

A customer usually buys goods or services from a business.

Examples:

  • The shop has many customers.
  • The customer bought a new phone.

A client usually receives professional or ongoing services.

Examples:

  • The lawyer met with her client.
  • The company has several international clients.

So, a person buying vegetables from a grocery store is normally a customer, while someone receiving legal advice is usually a client.

15. Avoid Misplaced Modifiers

A modifier is a word or phrase that gives more information about another word.

The modifier should be placed close to the word it describes. Otherwise, the meaning can become confusing.

Compare these sentences:

Unclear: She almost drove her kids to school every day.

This can suggest that she nearly drove them to school but did not actually do it.

Clear: She drove her kids to school almost every day.

Now it is clear that she actually drove them to school, but she did not do so every day.

Another example:

Unclear: I saw a man walking down the street with a telescope.

Who has the telescope, the man or the speaker?

Clear writing places words carefully so that the reader does not have to guess.

16. Your vs. You're

These words sound the same, but they have different meanings.

Your shows possession.

You're means “you are.”

Examples:

  • Your book is on the table.
  • Is this your pen?
  • You're very good at English.
  • You're late for class.

A simple trick:

If you can replace the word with “you are,” use you're.

17. Their vs. There vs. They're

These three words are often confused.

Their shows possession.

  • Their house is large.
  • The students finished their homework.

There refers to a place or introduces something.

  • The book is over there.
  • There are five students in the room.

They're means “they are.”

  • They're coming home.
  • They're ready for the exam.

Remember:

Their = possession

There = place or introduction

They're = they are

18. Much vs. Many

Use “many” with countable nouns and “much” with uncountable nouns.

Examples:

  • How many books do you have?
  • How many students are in your class?
  • How much water do you drink?
  • How much money do you need?

So:

Many + countable nouns

Much + uncountable nouns

In everyday positive sentences, “a lot of” is often more natural than “much.”

For example:

  • I have a lot of homework.
  • She has a lot of friends.

19. Good vs. Well

“Good” is usually an adjective. It describes a noun.

“Well” is usually an adverb. It describes how an action is performed.

Examples:

  • She is a good student.
  • He is a good player.
  • She sings well.
  • He writes well.

Compare:

She is a good singer.

Here, “good” describes the noun “singer.”

She sings well.

Here, “well” describes the verb “sings.”

20. Since vs. For

Use “since” to mention the starting point of an action or situation.

Use “for” to mention the length of time.

Examples:

  • I have lived here since 2020.
  • She has been sick since Monday.
  • I have lived here for six years.
  • He has studied English for three hours.

Remember:

Since + starting point

For + period of time

For example:

  • since 2020
  • since Monday
  • since morning

But:

  • for two years
  • for three days
  • for five hours

How to Avoid These Grammar Mistakes

Knowing the rules is only the first step. You also need to use them regularly.

Here are some simple habits that can help:

  1. Read English every day. Read books, newspapers, articles, or good educational websites.

  2. Write a few sentences every day. Try writing about your school, family, hobbies, or daily activities.

  3. Check your common mistakes. If you repeatedly make the same mistake, write the correct form in a notebook.

  4. Learn grammar through examples. Instead of memorizing a rule alone, study several sentences that show how the rule works.

  5. Read your writing again. Many grammar mistakes become obvious when you read your work slowly.

  6. Practice speaking. Grammar improves when you use English regularly, not only when you study for an exam.

  7. Do not worry about every mistake while speaking. The goal is to communicate clearly first. Accuracy improves with practice.

Final Thoughts

Grammar is not about memorizing hundreds of complicated rules. It is about understanding how words work together and using them correctly.

Some mistakes, such as “its” and “it's” or “then” and “than,” can be fixed by learning a simple rule. Other areas, such as “who” and “whom” or “between” and “among,” depend on the context.

If you are preparing for a Class 10 English exam, focus on understanding the rule and then practice it in your own sentences. Do not simply memorize the examples above. Try creating new examples yourself.

The more you read, write, listen, and speak in English, the easier correct grammar will become.
