15 Common English Grammar Mistakes You Might Be Making (And How to Fix Them)

August 21, 2026 by Study Koro · 4 min read

Mastering English grammar can be tricky, even for fluent speakers. Small errors often sneak into everyday conversations and writing without us realizing it. In a recent grammar breakdown, Munzereen Shahid highlighted some of the most frequent grammar missteps and explained how to fix them effortlessly.

Here is a guide to the most common mistakes covered in the lesson and how you can avoid them:

1. Expressing Hypothetical Wishes: "I wish I was..." vs. "I wish I were..."

  • Incorrect: I wish I was a cat.

  • Correct: I wish I were a cat.

Why: When expressing hypothetical situations, unreal conditions, or wishes, use the subjunctive mood (were) regardless of whether the subject is singular or plural.

2. Standard Exam Terminology: "Giving an exam" vs. "Taking an exam"

  • Incorrect: The student gave the exam.

  • Correct: The student took the exam.

Why: From the student's perspective, they take or sit for an exam, whereas the teacher gives or administers it.

3. Using Unnecessary Prepositions: "Enter into"

  • Incorrect: We entered into the classroom.

  • Correct: We entered the classroom.

Why: The verb enter inherently includes the movement into a physical space. However, note that "entered into" is acceptable when referring to abstract concepts like discussions, agreements, or debates (e.g., "We entered into an agreement").

4. Overusing Articles with Uncountable Nouns & Languages

  • Incorrect: The gold is a precious metal. / He speaks the English.

  • Correct: Gold is a precious metal. / He speaks English.

Why: General material nouns (gold, silver) and languages do not require the article "the". However, "the English" can be used when referring to the people/nation as a group.

5. Redundant Phrasing: "Returned back"

  • Incorrect: She returned back to school.

  • Correct: She returned to school. OR She came back to school.

Why: The word return already means "to go back." Adding "back" makes the sentence redundant.

6. Distinguishing Between "Between" and "Among"

  • Incorrect: The negotiations are between the three countries.

  • Correct: The negotiations are among the three countries.

Why: Use between when referring to two distinct items/parties, and among when referring to three or more.

7. Choosing Comparison Words: "Then" vs. "Than"

  • Incorrect: I would rather have cake then ice cream.

  • Correct: I would rather have cake than ice cream.

Why: Use than (with an a) for comparisons. Then (with an e) refers to time sequence (e.g., "We ate dinner, then left").

8. Countable vs. Uncountable Comparisons: "Fewer" vs. "Less"

  • Incorrect: I have less books than you.

  • Correct: I have fewer books than you.

Why: Use fewer for countable nouns (books, cars, apples) and less for uncountable nouns (water, sugar, salt).

9. Confusion Over Possessives and Contractions: "Its" vs. "It's"

  • Incorrect: The cat licked it's paw.

  • Correct: The cat licked its paw.

Why: It's is a contraction for it is or it has. Its (without an apostrophe) is the possessive pronoun.

10. Avoid Double Negatives

  • Incorrect: I don't need no help.

  • Correct: I don't need any help. OR I need no help.

Why: Combining two negatives creates a positive statement, leading to confusion or non-standard grammar.

11. Choosing the Right Subject Pronoun: "Who" vs. "Whom"

  • Incorrect: Whom is calling?

  • Correct: Who is calling?

Why: Who functions as a subject pronoun (he, she, they), while whom functions as an object pronoun (him, her, them).

12. Prepositions with Locations: "Go home" vs. "Go to home"

  • Incorrect: I want to go to home.

  • Correct: I want to go home.

Why: In this context, home acts as an adverb of direction, so the preposition to is omitted. However, specific named places do require to (e.g., "I want to go to Paris").

13. Word Choice: "Center" vs. "Middle"

  • Incorrect: He is in the center of the road.

  • Correct: He is in the middle of the road.

Why: Use center for precise geometric central points (like a circle or square) and middle for general areas, physical paths, or linear spaces (road, room, bus).

14. Differentiating Business Relationships: "Clients" vs. "Customers"

  • Incorrect: The grocer has many clients.

  • Correct: The grocer has many customers.

Why: Customers buy goods from shops or retail stores, while clients receive professional services (e.g., lawyers, consultants).

15. Understanding Misplaced Modifiers

  • Incorrect: She almost drove her kids to school every day.

  • Correct: She drove her kids to school almost every day.

Why: Place modifiers as close as possible to the words they modify. Placing "almost" before "drove" implies she nearly drove but didn't actually do it!

Conclusion

Grammar mistakes happen to everyone, but practicing these quick fixes will significantly boost your confidence in spoken and written English.
