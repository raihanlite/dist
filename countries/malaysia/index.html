Study in Malaysia from Bangladesh

19 Nov 2026 · 14 min read · Last checked 19 Nov 2026

Malaysia is one of the closest and most affordable study destinations for Bangladeshi students. Degrees are taught in English, tuition at public universities is far below the UK or Australia, and the climate, food and culture feel familiar. But Malaysia has rules that surprise many Bangladeshi students: you cannot work during term time, and the post-study Graduate Pass does not currently include Bangladeshi citizens. This guide gives you the verified facts, including the ones that work against you, so you can plan honestly.

Why study in Malaysia?

The strongest reason is value for money in English. Almost all degree programmes for international students are taught in English, so you do not need to learn a new language to study, unlike in China or Germany. Public universities such as Universiti Malaya — ranked 56th in the QS World University Rankings 2027 — charge international students far less than a similarly ranked university in the UK or Australia, and private universities and foreign branch campuses (including Australian and British universities with Malaysian campuses) give you a foreign degree without leaving Asia.

Malaysia is also close and familiar. The flight from Dhaka to Kuala Lumpur is short, the food and climate are similar to home, there is a large Bangladeshi community, and halal food is everywhere. For many families, that makes Malaysia feel safer than a faraway destination for a first degree.

The trade-offs matter too. Part-time work is far more restricted than students expect — there is no term-time work at all — so you cannot earn your way through a degree. And after graduation, the main post-study route is closed to Bangladeshi citizens for now, so your plan should be to return home with your degree, or to secure a sponsored job while your Student Pass is still valid. If those two limits are acceptable, Malaysia is a strong, affordable choice. If you were counting on working during your studies or staying on after graduation, look at another country first.

Tuition and living costs

Tuition is set by each university and varies by programme, so always check the exact fee on the university's own website or in your offer letter. The examples below are published fees for international students, to help you judge the range.

University / typeExample published tuitionNotes
Universiti Malaya (public)About RM 17,000–RM 33,000 per yearWhole-programme fees of about RM 59,400 (humanities) to RM 132,100 (engineering) for a bachelor's degree. Medicine is far higher, about RM 134,640 a year.
Asia Pacific University (private)About RM 54,200–RM 93,500 for a whole bachelor's programmeListed on the Education Malaysia portal. Works out to roughly RM 18,000–RM 31,000 a year over a 3-year degree.
Foreign branch campusesHigher than local private universitiesA UK or Australian branch campus in Malaysia costs more than a local university, but less than the same degree in the UK or Australia.

That is why a planning range of about RM 20,000–RM 40,000 a year fits many programmes — but it is only a planning range. Medicine, dentistry, pharmacy and some engineering and business programmes can cost much more, and a 6% service tax is charged on top of tuition for non-citizens at some universities. Foundation and diploma programmes are usually cheaper than a full degree. Get the fee in writing in your offer letter before you pay anything.

For living costs, there is no official national figure, and the city matters. Kuala Lumpur is the most expensive city; living in Penang, Johor Bahru or a smaller university town costs less. Your main costs are accommodation, food, transport, phone and internet, and books. Many students share a room or a flat near campus to keep rent down. Do not trust a single "monthly living cost" number from an agent — build your own budget from the university's accommodation prices and a realistic food and transport allowance for that city.

Admission requirements

Admission is decided by each university, not by a central system, so requirements differ by programme. The pattern below is typical for Bangladeshi applicants.

By level

  • Foundation / diploma: SSC, sometimes with a minimum GPA. A foundation year is common if your HSC subjects do not match the degree you want.
  • Bachelor's: HSC (or equivalent) with the subjects and grades the programme asks for. Some programmes add an interview or a test.
  • Master's: a bachelor's degree in a related field. Universiti Malaya, for example, asks for a CGPA of at least 3.00 for most master's programmes, plus any programme-specific requirements.
  • PhD: a master's degree in a related field and a research proposal.

English

Most degree programmes are taught in English, and most universities ask for proof of English. The usual range is IELTS 5.5–6.5 overall, depending on the level and the programme — foundation and diploma programmes accept lower scores, and medicine, law and English-language programmes ask for more. As an example, Universiti Malaya asks for IELTS 5.0 for most bachelor's programmes and IELTS 6.0 for most postgraduate programmes, and also accepts TOEFL, PTE and MUET (the Malaysian University English Test) at equivalent levels. Some universities accept a medium-of-instruction letter from your previous university instead of a test, especially for postgraduate study — check the programme page, because this varies.

When to apply

Many Malaysian universities have two main intakes a year, and some have three. Application windows and deadlines differ by university and programme, so check the university's own admissions page. Start 6–9 months before you want to begin, because the EMGS student-pass process takes weeks after you accept your offer. Never pay an agent for a "guaranteed" place — apply through the university's own portal.

Scholarships

Malaysia's government scholarships are for postgraduate study, not for bachelor's degrees. That is an important limit: if you are applying for a bachelor's degree, your funding will come from university scholarships, not from the government.

ScholarshipWho it is forWhat it covers
Malaysia International Scholarship (MIS)International students for master's and PhD studyTuition fees and a monthly living allowance (about RM 1,500 a month)
MTCP ScholarshipStudents from developing countries, including Bangladesh, for a full-time master's degreeFull tuition, monthly allowance of RM 3,500, and a return economy air ticket
University scholarshipsStudents admitted to that university, often for strong gradesUsually a partial tuition waiver; amounts vary by university

The MTCP Scholarship is the more generous of the two government awards. It is run by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs under the Malaysian Technical Cooperation Programme, and it is open to citizens of MTCP recipient countries, which include Bangladesh. Applicants must be no more than 45 years old, hold a bachelor's degree with at least Second Class Upper honours or a CGPA of 3.00, meet the English requirement (for example IELTS 6.0), and hold an offer from a participating Malaysian public university. It funds a full-time master's degree only — not a bachelor's or a PhD — and some fields, such as nursing, medicine and clinical pharmacy, are not eligible. The MIS, run by the Ministry of Higher Education, is for master's and PhD study and is open to international students more broadly.

Both scholarships open once a year and their deadlines change every cycle, so check the official portals for the current dates rather than trusting a blog. Apply for admission first: the MTCP scholarship normally requires a confirmed offer from a Malaysian university before you apply. University scholarships are worth checking programme by programme — many private universities offer merit awards of 10–50% of tuition, and public universities have smaller awards for strong students.

Student visa: the Student Pass and EMGS

Malaysia does not work like the UK or USA, where you apply for a visa at an embassy. Every international student on a full-time course needs a Student Pass, issued by the Immigration Department of Malaysia, and the application runs through EMGS (Education Malaysia Global Services), the agency owned by the Ministry of Higher Education. Your university submits the application on your behalf — you supply the documents and pay the fees, but you do not apply at an embassy yourself.

The steps are:

  1. Apply to the university, receive an offer, and accept it by paying any deposit or first-semester fee the university asks for.
  2. Send your documents to the university's international office. The university uploads them and submits your Student Pass application through EMGS.
  3. EMGS checks your file and sends it to the Immigration Department, which issues an electronic Visa Approval Letter (eVAL). The eVAL is valid for six months from issue, so book your travel inside that window.
  4. Depending on your nationality, you may also need a Single Entry Visa before you fly — check the EMGS country list, and apply through the eVISA portal or a Malaysian mission if it applies to you.
  5. After you arrive, complete a medical screening at an EMGS panel clinic within 7 days, and your Student Pass is then endorsed in your passport. The pass is usually issued for up to 12 months at a time and renewed each year.

EMGS publishes an official schedule of fees. For a new student on a programme of more than 6 and up to 12 months at a university in Peninsular Malaysia, the processing fee is RM 1,600, plus 8% service tax; the eVAL fee is RM 150, plus 8% tax. Other charges in the schedule include medical screening (RM 250), medical insurance (from about RM 456 a year, depending on the plan), the Student Pass sticker (RM 60) and the i-Kad (RM 50). Added together, first-year EMGS and immigration costs commonly come to about RM 2,700–RM 3,300 before university charges, plus a refundable personal bond — the amount varies by nationality, and sources citing EMGS put the bond for Bangladeshi students at about RM 750. Use the EMGS fee calculator on the official portal for your exact total, because fees differ for East Malaysia and for some institution types.

EMGS's client charter is 14 working days for a complete application — but that is only the EMGS stage, and only for a complete file. In practice, plan on about three months from offer to arrival, because the university checks, the eVAL, the Single Entry Visa (if needed) and travel booking all take time. Incomplete documents restart the clock, so check every document before your university submits. Your passport needs at least 18 months of validity when you apply. Malaysia has no blocked-account rule: instead of a fixed sum in a blocked account, EMGS and your university assess whether you can pay your fees and living costs, so keep your bank statements and any scholarship letter ready.

Watch out: do not enter Malaysia while your Student Pass application is still being processed, and do not start work before your pass is endorsed. Both can cause serious problems with Immigration. Wait for the eVAL, travel, complete the medical screening, and get your pass endorsed first.

Working while studying

This is the rule most students get wrong, so read it carefully. International students in Malaysia may work part-time for a maximum of 20 hours a week, only during semester breaks or holidays of more than 7 consecutive days. There is no term-time work. Even during a break, you need prior approval from the Immigration Department, applied for through your university — an employer's offer letter alone is not permission to start work.

Work is also limited to approved sectors: restaurants, petrol stations, mini markets and hotels, plus work inside university or college areas. Some roles are banned, including cashier work, front-desk duties, security work and work as a student recruitment agent. Language-centre and training-centre students are not eligible for this permission at all — it is for Student Pass holders at public universities and private higher education institutions.

Part-time work in Malaysia will not cover your tuition, and it should never be part of your funding plan. Budget as if you will earn nothing while you study. Working without approval, or working during term time, can lead to your Student Pass being cancelled.

Staying after graduation

Malaysia does have a post-study route — the Graduate Pass, a 12-month Social Visit Pass that lets graduates of Malaysian universities stay for a year to look for work or travel, and work part-time in permitted sectors without an employer sponsor. But here is the fact that matters for you: Bangladesh is not currently on the list of eligible nationalities for the Graduate Pass. The list covers 32 countries, and Bangladeshi citizens are not among them. The list can change, so check the current EMGS list before you rely on it — but do not plan your future on it.

For a Bangladeshi graduate, the realistic route to stay is to secure a job offer from a Malaysian employer while your Student Pass is still valid, and move directly to an Employment Pass. That needs an employer registered with the Expatriate Services Division and a salary that meets the Employment Pass threshold for the category — these thresholds were raised in 2026, so check the current figures with the employer. Start looking for work well before your final semester ends, because the window is short. Most Bangladeshi graduates should plan to return home and use their degree there, or to move on to further study or a job in another country.

Documents checklist

  • Valid passport (at least 18 months' validity when you apply)
  • Offer letter from the university
  • Academic transcripts and certificates (SSC, HSC, bachelor's if applicable)
  • English test result (IELTS / TOEFL / PTE / MUET), or a medium-of-instruction letter where the university accepts one
  • Passport-sized photographs, as EMGS specifies
  • Completed EMGS application forms and health declaration form
  • Financial proof (bank statements or a scholarship letter showing you can pay tuition and living costs)
  • Medical report, if the university or EMGS asks for one before travel
  • Health insurance covering the full Student Pass period
  • Single Entry Visa (eVISA), if your nationality needs one — check the EMGS list
