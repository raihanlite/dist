Study in Japan from Bangladesh

19 Nov 2026 · 12 min read · Last checked 19 Nov 2026

Japan is one of the most affordable places in the developed world to get a degree. National universities charge the same tuition to foreign students as to Japanese students — ¥535,800 a year (about ৳4,18,000) — and the government's MEXT scholarship pays tuition, a monthly stipend and airfare in full. The trade-off is language: most undergraduate teaching is in Japanese, and daily life runs in Japanese too.

Why study in Japan?

The first reason is price. A national university degree in Japan costs roughly a tenth of a similar degree in the USA, and there is no international-student surcharge: the ¥535,800 tuition is the same for everyone. Private universities cost more, but even they are usually cheaper than British or American universities.

The second reason is the MEXT scholarship. Japan's government scholarship is one of the most generous in the world: full tuition, a monthly living stipend and return airfare. It is competitive, but unlike many scholarships it is open to Bangladeshi students through the Embassy of Japan in Dhaka, and it covers the whole degree.

The third reason is safety and order. Japan is very safe, public transport is reliable and on time, and universities are well organised. Many Bangladeshi students also value the part-time work culture: with permission, you can work up to 28 hours a week in shops, restaurants and convenience stores.

The honest downside is language. Outside a few English-taught programmes, you will study in Japanese, sit exams in Japanese and look for jobs in Japanese. Most students need one to two years of serious Japanese study before or during their degree. If you are not willing to learn Japanese, Japan is a hard choice.

Tuition and living costs

Japan has three kinds of university, and the price gap between them is large.

The national-university figure is a standard rate set by the government (MEXT). A national university may charge up to 20% above it, so a few charge ¥642,960 a year — always check the fee page of the university you apply to. Private universities set their own fees, and many add facility and laboratory charges on top of tuition.

Living costs vary by city. Tokyo is the most expensive; smaller cities such as Fukuoka, Sendai or Sapporo are cheaper. A JASSO survey of privately financed international students put average monthly spending at about ¥105,000 (about ৳82,000), not counting study and research costs — rent is the biggest item, then food, transport and National Health Insurance. Plan on ¥100,000–130,000 a month and check the cost-of-living page for your city before you budget.

A MEXT scholar pays almost none of this: tuition and the admission fee are waived, and the monthly stipend covers living costs.

Admission requirements

Japan's school system is 12 years, the same as Bangladesh, so an HSC certificate normally meets the schooling requirement for a bachelor's degree. Graduate admission needs a bachelor's degree in a related field. Each university sets its own entrance rules, so the steps below are the usual pattern, not a guarantee.

Japanese-taught programmes (most bachelor's degrees)

You usually need Japanese at JLPT N2 level or above, and many universities also ask for the EJU — the Examination for Japanese University Admission for International Students — which tests Japanese, science or humanities subjects, and mathematics. Some universities run their own entrance exams instead. If your Japanese is not yet at N2, the usual path is a Japanese language school first, or a MEXT undergraduate place that includes a one-year preparatory Japanese course.

English-taught programmes

A growing number of universities offer full degrees in English, mostly at master's level and in a smaller number of bachelor's programmes. These usually ask for IELTS 6.0–6.5 or TOEFL equivalent, plus your transcripts and a statement of purpose. You will still need everyday Japanese for life outside the classroom, and for most jobs after graduation.

When to apply

The Japanese academic year starts in April, and most universities also have an October intake. Applications typically open 6–12 months before the course starts, and deadlines differ by university and programme. MEXT applications through the embassy open roughly a year before the course starts — check the Embassy of Japan in Dhaka's notice for the current round.

MEXT is the scholarship to aim for, but it is competitive and slow: the embassy route involves a written exam and an interview, and the whole process takes about a year. Apply through the Embassy of Japan in Dhaka — never through an agent who charges a fee for MEXT, because the application is free.

The JASSO Honors Scholarship is different: you cannot apply for it from Bangladesh. Your university recommends you after you enrol, so it is help for your second year onwards, not for your first-year budget. It also does not cover tuition — it is a living-cost stipend only.

Two honest warnings. First, MEXT stipend amounts can change with the Japanese government's budget — the application guidelines say so plainly, so confirm the current figure before you plan around it. Second, a few universities have tightened tuition exemptions for international students from the 2027 academic year, so do not assume a waiver you saw on an old page still exists.

Student visa

Japan's student visa has two stages, and the first one takes the longest.

Stage 1: Certificate of Eligibility (COE)

After you are accepted, your school applies on your behalf to Japan's Immigration Services Agency for a Certificate of Eligibility — a document that confirms you meet the conditions for a student visa. The standard processing time is 1 to 3 months, and there is no fee for the COE. Your school will ask you for academic records, proof of funds from you or your sponsor, and sometimes a study plan. Start this step early: the COE is the part that most often delays a student's arrival.

Stage 2: the visa itself

Once you have the COE, you apply for the visa in Dhaka. Since 3 November 2024, the Embassy of Japan in Dhaka accepts visa applications only through VFS Global in Gulshan-2 — not at the embassy directly. You book an appointment online, submit your passport, the COE, the application form and a photo, and the embassy's standard processing time is about 7 working days from the day VFS receives a complete application.

Visa fee: Japan raised its general visa fees from 1 July 2026 to about ¥15,000 for a single-entry visa (about ৳11,700). The Embassy of Japan in Dhaka's older pages list the visa as free for Bangladeshi nationals, but those pages were written before the change, and the fee that applies to Bangladeshi applicants now could not be confirmed at an official source. Confirm the current fee with the embassy or VFS Global before you pay. VFS Global also charges its own service fee.

Financial proof: there is no single published amount for Bangladeshi applicants. The embassy checks that you or your sponsor can cover tuition and living costs — show clear bank statements and, if a family member is your sponsor, proof of their income and your relationship. MEXT scholars use their scholarship letter instead.

Working while studying

A student visa on its own does not allow you to work. Before you take any job, you need shikakugai katsudō kyoka — permission to engage in activity outside your visa status. The permission is free, and students arriving for the first time on a student visa of more than three months can often get it stamped at the airport.

With permission, you may work up to 28 hours a week during term time, and up to 8 hours a day during long school holidays. The 28 hours are your total across all jobs, not per job. Work in adult-entertainment businesses is prohibited, even for jobs like cleaning or serving drinks there. Working without permission is illegal employment and can cost you your visa — some students have had to leave Japan because part-time work damaged their attendance or broke the rules.

Common student jobs are convenience stores, restaurants, supermarkets, hotels and tutoring. Pay depends on the city — Tokyo's minimum wage is higher than in smaller cities. Part-time earnings help, but they are not enough to pay tuition and rent on their own: plan your budget as if the work does not exist, and treat earnings as a bonus.

Staying after graduation

If you graduate without a job offer, you can change from Student status to Designated Activities for continued job hunting. The permit is granted for 6 months and can be renewed once, so you can stay for up to 1 year after graduation. You need a recommendation letter from your graduating university and proof that you were already job hunting before graduation, and you must apply before your student status expires. A second year is possible only through an approved local-government job-support programme. With permission, you can work part-time up to 28 hours a week while you search.

If you find a job, you change to a work status of residence — most commonly Engineer / Specialist in Humanities / International Services. Your employer confirms the offer, and the job should match your degree. Japanese employers hire on their own calendar, with many graduates starting work in April, so begin your job search in your second-to-last year, not after graduation.

There is also a separate route, J-Find, for graduates of universities ranked in the top 100 of at least two of the three main world rankings. It allows up to 2 years in Japan to look for work or prepare a start-up. It does not apply to most graduates, so treat it as a bonus, not a plan.

One thing to be clear about: a Japanese degree does not guarantee a job in Japan. Japanese language ability decides most hiring outcomes — graduates with JLPT N1 have far more options than those without it.

Documents checklist

  • Valid passport
  • Offer or admission letter from the Japanese school
  • Certificate of Eligibility (COE), applied for by your school in Japan
  • Visa application form and a recent photo (as specified by the embassy)
  • Academic transcripts and certificates (SSC, HSC, bachelor's if applicable)
  • Language proof: JLPT certificate or EJU result for Japanese-taught programmes; IELTS/TOEFL for English-taught programmes
  • Financial proof: bank statements and sponsor documents, or MEXT scholarship letter
  • Study plan or statement of purpose (as the school or embassy asks)
  • VFS Global appointment booking (applications go through VFS in Gulshan-2, not the embassy directly)
  • National Health Insurance enrolment — done at your city or ward office after arrival; it is mandatory for residents
