Study in China from Bangladesh

19 Nov 2026 · 13 min read · Last checked 19 Nov 2026

China is one of the most affordable study destinations for Bangladeshi students — if you plan it well. Tuition at many public universities is far below the UK, USA or Australia, and the Chinese Government Scholarship (CSC) can cover tuition, housing and a monthly stipend in full. But China is not a cheap shortcut: most degree programmes are taught in Chinese, part-time work is tightly controlled, and there is no general post-study work visa. This guide gives you the verified facts, so you can decide with clear eyes.

Why study in China?

China has several universities ranked among the best in the world, including Peking University and Tsinghua University, and it is strong in engineering, medicine, computer science, business and natural sciences. For a Bangladeshi student, the main attractions are cost and funding: self-funded tuition at a good public university can be a quarter of what a UK university charges, and a CSC scholarship removes tuition and housing costs altogether.

There are real trade-offs. Most bachelor's programmes are taught in Chinese, so without Mandarin your choice of programmes is much smaller — mainly some engineering, business, economics and medicine programmes taught in English, concentrated at the postgraduate level. Daily life also runs in Chinese outside the big international campuses, so learning some Mandarin before you go makes everyday life far easier, even on an English-taught programme.

China also suits students who want a degree with strong laboratory, hospital or industry links. Engineering and medical programmes often come with well-equipped facilities and large teaching hospitals. If your goal is a degree in English, a low cost, and a scholarship, China is worth a serious look. If your goal is to work part-time to pay your fees, or to stay and work after graduation, it is a much weaker choice — be honest with yourself about that before you apply.

Tuition and living costs

China has no national fee schedule for international students. Each university sets its own tuition, programme by programme, so the exact fee is always on the university's own admissions page. The published fees below are examples from major universities, to help you judge the range — they are not quotes for your programme.

UniversityExample published tuition per yearNotes
Tsinghua University¥26,000–¥40,000Undergraduate, most Chinese-taught subjects. English-taught undergraduate programmes cost far more.
Peking University¥26,000–¥30,000Undergraduate humanities and sciences. Master's and PhD fees are higher.
Wuhan University¥23,000–¥28,000English-taught bachelor's degrees outside medicine.
Zhejiang University¥19,800–¥29,800Chinese-taught bachelor's degrees.

Medicine costs more everywhere: clinical medicine at top universities can reach ¥40,000–¥70,000 a year, and English-taught medicine at some universities is higher still. Smaller provincial universities are often cheaper than the famous Beijing and Shanghai names — some charge under ¥20,000 a year for a bachelor's degree. Always check the fee for your exact programme and language of instruction, because an English-taught version of the same degree can cost much more than the Chinese-taught one.

On top of tuition, budget for medical insurance (about ¥800 a year at Tsinghua, for example), an application fee (usually a few hundred yuan), accommodation if it is not covered by a scholarship, food, transport and books. There is no official national living-cost figure for international students, and city differences are large: a dormitory bed and canteen meals in a smaller city cost far less than a room and eating out in Beijing or Shanghai. Do not trust a single "monthly living cost" number from an agent or a blog — build your own budget from the university's dormitory price and a realistic food and transport allowance for that city.

Admission requirements

Admission is decided by each university, not by a central system, so requirements differ. The pattern below is typical for Bangladeshi applicants.

By level

  • Bachelor's: HSC (or equivalent) with good grades in subjects related to your chosen programme. Some universities also ask for an entrance test or interview.
  • Master's: a bachelor's degree in a related field, transcripts, a study plan or research proposal, and usually two recommendation letters.
  • PhD: a master's degree in a related field, a research proposal, and a supervisor or department willing to take you.

Language

There are two tracks, and the language test you need depends on which one you choose.

TrackTypical language requirementWho it suits
English-taught programmeIELTS 6.0–6.5 overall, or TOEFL 80–90. Some top programmes ask for more.Students with strong English and little or no Chinese. Choice is widest at master's and PhD level.
Chinese-taught programmeHSK (Chinese proficiency test) — the CSC scholarship asks for HSK 3 for a bachelor's degree and HSK 4 for master's and PhD study; individual universities may ask for more.Students who already know Chinese, or who take a one-year Chinese language course first.

Many universities offer a one-year Chinese language (preparatory) course before a Chinese-taught degree. If you do not yet have the HSK level you need, that year is a realistic route — but it adds a year of costs, so include it in your budget. Since the 2026/27 cycle, bachelor's applicants for the CSC scholarship have also had to take a new test, the CSCA (Chinese University Entrance Test for International Students), before applying — check the current requirement on campuschina.org, because this is a recent change.

When to apply

Most universities open applications in the autumn for entry the following September, and many close between December and June depending on the programme. Scholarship deadlines fall much earlier than self-funded deadlines, so start 10–12 months before you want to begin. Never pay an application fee to an agent for a "guaranteed" place — apply through the university's own admissions page and the CSC portal.

Scholarships

Scholarships are the main reason China is affordable for many Bangladeshi students. The CSC scholarship is the big one, but it is not the only one.

ScholarshipWho it is forWhat it covers
Chinese Government Scholarship (CSC)International students at all degree levels, including bachelor's, master's and PhDFull tuition, free university dormitory or accommodation subsidy, monthly stipend, comprehensive medical insurance
University scholarshipsStudents admitted to that university, often for strong gradesUsually a full or partial tuition waiver; some add a stipend
Provincial and city government scholarshipsInternational students at universities in that province or cityUsually a partial tuition waiver or a one-off award

The CSC scholarship is run by the China Scholarship Council and applied for through campuschina.org. There are two main routes: Type A, through the Chinese Embassy in Bangladesh (the bilateral programme), and Type B, directly through a university. Both use the same online system. The age limits published by Chinese embassies are under 25 for a bachelor's degree, under 35 for a master's, and under 40 for a PhD. The monthly stipend published by Chinese embassies is CNY 2,500 for undergraduates, CNY 3,000 for master's students and general scholars, and CNY 3,500 for doctoral students and senior scholars — about ৳47,000, ৳56,000 and ৳65,000 a month at the 19 Nov 2026 rate. Some Type A awards also include return airfare; check the embassy's notice for your cycle, because that detail varies.

CSC is competitive, and a scholarship does not guarantee admission to your first-choice university or programme. Apply to the scholarship and to universities in parallel, and keep a self-funded plan as a backup. University scholarships are worth checking programme by programme: many universities publish their own awards on their international admissions pages, and some are open to students who miss out on CSC. Deadlines change every year — the embassy in Dhaka publishes its own notice and deadline for Type A each cycle, so that notice, not a blog, is your deadline.

Student visa

Bangladeshi passport holders need a visa before travelling to China. There is no visa-free entry or visa on arrival for study. Applications are made through the Chinese Visa Application Service Center (CVASC) in Dhaka, after completing the online form.

Visa typeForMain documents
X1Study of more than 180 days (almost all degree programmes)Admission letter, Form JW201 or JW202 (Visa Application for Study in China), passport, form and photo
X2Study of no more than 180 days (short courses, language study)Admission letter, passport, form and photo

Your university sends you the admission letter and the JW201 or JW202 form after you accept your offer — you cannot apply for the visa without them, so do not book flights before they arrive. After you enter China on an X1 visa, you must apply for a study residence permit at the local public security bureau within 30 days of arrival. The X1 visa itself is only your entry document; the residence permit is what lets you stay for your whole programme.

The consular fee for a single-entry visa for Bangladeshi citizens is about BDT 2,400, with higher fees for double and multiple entries. That is the consular fee only: CVASC charges its own service fee on top, so the total you pay at the centre is higher. Confirm the current total at the visa centre before you pay, because fees change and the service fee moves with the exchange rate.

On financial proof, be careful: some Chinese embassies publish a figure — for example, proof of personal assets of at least USD 2,500 for a year for an X1 visa, or proof of a scholarship instead. The Dhaka centre may ask for a different amount or different documents, so treat USD 2,500 as a planning guide only and confirm the exact requirement at the CVASC in Dhaka before your appointment. A scholarship award letter counts as financial proof if you have one. Also expect a medical examination: X1 applicants are usually asked for one, and the requirements are set by the embassy and the university.

Watch out: your visa does not let you work. A student visa or residence permit is not a work permit, and working without the proper endorsement is illegal employment — it can lead to fines, detention and deportation. See the next section before you accept any paid work.

Working while studying

China's work rules for international students are strict, and many students misunderstand them. The basic rule is simple: a student residence permit does not give you a general right to work. There are only two lawful routes, and both need your university's approval.

  • On-campus work-study: jobs organised and managed by your university, such as library or research-assistant work. National rules set a general limit of no more than 8 hours a week and 40 hours a month during term time. Your university publishes its own rules and manages the posts.
  • Off-campus internships or part-time work: only after your university approves it, and only if the exit-entry authority adds an endorsement to your residence permit stating the place and period of the work. Without that endorsement, off-campus work — paid or unpaid — is illegal employment.

Private tutoring, especially teaching English for cash, is one of the fastest ways to get into trouble: it is illegal employment without the endorsement, and students have been fined and deported for it. Part-time work in China will not cover your tuition, and it should never be part of your funding plan. Budget as if you will earn nothing while you study, and treat any approved work-study income as a small bonus.

Staying after graduation

Be honest about this before you commit: China has no general post-study work visa like the UK's Graduate Route or Canada's post-graduation work permit. Your student residence permit does not turn into a work permit when you graduate. To stay and work, you need a job offer from an employer that can sponsor you, and a work permit and work-type residence permit — the same route as any other foreign worker.

There is one helpful exception. Under a national notice, outstanding foreign graduates — including master's degree holders and above from Chinese universities — can apply for a work permit within one year of graduation without the usual two years of work experience. Some cities, such as Shanghai, have their own schemes for graduates of well-known universities. These routes are narrow, competitive and tied to specific employers and occupations, so do not count on them. Most Bangladeshi graduates should plan either to return home and use their degree there, or to move on to further study or a job elsewhere. Check the current rules at your university's international office and the local exit-entry authority in your final year — rules for graduates change, and city schemes differ.

Documents checklist

  • Valid passport (at least 6 months beyond your planned stay, with blank pages)
  • HSC certificate and transcript (for bachelor's), or bachelor's/master's degree and transcripts (for postgraduate study)
  • English test result (IELTS/TOEFL) for English-taught programmes, or HSK result for Chinese-taught programmes
  • Study plan or research proposal, and recommendation letters (mainly for master's and PhD)
  • Admission letter and Form JW201 or JW202 from your university
  • CSC scholarship award letter, if you have one
  • Financial proof (bank statement or scholarship letter, as the visa centre asks for)
  • Medical examination report, if the embassy or university asks for one
  • Visa application form, fee receipt and passport photos, as the CVASC specifies
  • Police clearance certificate, if your university or the visa centre asks for it
